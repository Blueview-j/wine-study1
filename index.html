Module 1

Types and styles of wine

Wine is an alcoholic drink made by fermenting grape juice. Every wine can be described by its type, its colour, how sweet it is and how heavy it feels.

Three types of wine

Still

The majority of wine. No bubbles; 8–15% alcohol, mostly 11.5–14%. Often named after the region (Sancerre, Burgundy, Rioja, Chianti) or the grape (Pinot Grigio).

Chablis, Rioja, Sancerre

Sparkling

Bubbles of carbon dioxide are trapped in the wine, usually during a second fermentation in a sealed tank or bottle. They are released when the bottle is opened.

Champagne, Prosecco, Cava

Fortified

A grape spirit is added during or after fermentation, raising the alcohol to around 15–22%. Some are sweet, some are dry.

Port, Sherry

Colour

Wines are white, rosé or red. White wines are usually made from white grapes. Red wines are made from black grapes, and the colour comes from the skins. Rosé is made from black grapes that start fermenting with their skins but are drained off them after a short time, so it picks up only a little colour.

Sweetness

Sweetness comes from grape sugar left in the wine after fermentation. Level 1 uses three words: dry, medium and sweet. Most wines are dry: the yeast have turned all the sugar into alcohol. A medium wine (usually white or rosé) is made by removing the yeast before all the sugar is used, or by adding unfermented grape juice to a dry wine. A sweet wine is made from grapes so high in sugar that the yeast stop before eating it all, or, for fortified wines, by adding high-strength spirit that kills the yeast. Sweet wines need high acidity so they don’t taste cloying.

Watch out: “dry” means not sweet. A wine that makes your gums feel rough has high tannin, which is a different thing.

Body

Body is how heavy or rich a wine feels in your mouth: light, medium or full. Think of the difference between skimmed, semi-skimmed and whole milk. Alcohol is the biggest contributor, helped by sugar, tannin and intense flavour.

Structure, aromas and flavours

Alongside colour, Level 1 describes a wine by five structural characteristics, plus its aromas and flavours. A wine’s style is simply the combination of all of these.

Module 2

How wine is made

Almost everything you taste in a glass starts in the grape. The vineyard decides the raw material; the winemaker decides what to do with it.

Inside a grape

The vine’s year

Growing the grape

Every year a vine flowers in spring. Each flower becomes a small, hard, green grape, and through summer the grapes ripen: they swell with water, sugar rises, acidity falls and flavours become riper. White grapes turn from green to golden; black grapes turn from green to purple. Grapes are picked in late summer. (The sugar is made in the leaves from sunlight, a process called photosynthesis.)

Where grapes grow

Vines need the right amount of sunlight, heat and water, and a winter cold enough for them to rest. Most vineyards lie between about 30° and 50° north and south of the Equator. Cool climates, nearer the poles (such as Germany and northern France), have lower temperatures and weaker sunlight, and grapes can struggle to ripen; white grapes are commonly grown. Warm climates, nearer the Equator (such as parts of Australia and California), have higher temperatures and more intense sunlight, and grapes ripen consistently; black grapes are common. The colder it is, the lower the sugar and the higher the acidity; the warmer it is, the higher the sugar, the lower the acidity and the riper the flavours.

Fermentation

Fermentation turns grape juice into wine. Yeast feeds on the sugar in the juice and turns it into alcohol and carbon dioxide. It also creates new flavours. Fermentation finishes when all the sugar is used up, giving a dry wine, or the winemaker can stop it early to leave some sweetness.

Making white, rosé and red wine

    The big difference: white wines are pressed before fermentation, so only the juice ferments. Red wines ferment with their skins, then are drained and pressed after fermentation. That is where red wine gets its colour and tannin. Rosé starts fermenting with its skins too, but is drained off them early, so it takes only a little colour.

    Maturation: steel or oak?

    Stainless steel

    Airtight and neutral. Keeps the fresh, fruity flavours of the grape. Most light whites and rosés are made and stored this way and bottled young.

    Oak barrels

    Add flavours such as vanilla, smoke, cedar, cloves and coconut, especially when the barrels are new. They also let tiny amounts of oxygen in, softening reds. Oak chips or staves are a cheaper way to give oak flavour.

    Sweet, sparkling and fortified wines

    Fermentation normally runs until the yeast have eaten all the sugar, giving a dry wine. To keep sweetness, the yeast are removed early or unfermented grape juice is added (medium wines), or the grapes are so sugary that the yeast give up (sweet wines). Fortified wines have high-strength spirit added, raising alcohol to 15–22%; in sweet styles such as Port, the spirit kills the yeast before they have eaten all the sugar. The detail of how sparkling and fortified wines are made is covered at Level 2.

    Grapes and classic wines

    Level 1 focuses on four white and four black grape varieties. Learn what each tastes like, the climate it prefers and the famous places it grows. Then meet the named wines every Level 1 student should recognise.

    Place and grape

    Many European wines are named after the place, not the grape. Tap a place, then tap the grape it is made from.

    Named wines to know

    Many famous wines are known by name rather than grape. Level 1 expects you to recognise these, know their type and describe their style. Apart from Champagne, Burgundy and Bordeaux, they are not made from the principal grapes, so their grapes are background only.

    Module 4

    Storing and serving wine

    A good wine can be spoilt by poor storage or the wrong temperature. Most wines are made to drink young, so storage is about keeping them in good condition, not ageing them.

    Storing wine

    RuleWhy
    Keep it cool and at a constant temperatureHeat cooks the fruit flavours; temperature swings push and pull the cork.
    Keep it in the darkStrong light, especially sunlight, damages wine.
    Lay cork-sealed bottles on their sideKeeps the cork moist. A dry cork shrinks and lets air in, which spoils the wine. Screwcaps can stand up.
    Avoid vibration and strong smellsBoth can affect the wine over time.

    Serving temperatures

    Serving too warm makes the alcohol obvious and the wine taste flabby. Serving too cold hides the flavour, and in reds makes the tannins taste harsher and more bitter. Level 1 uses four descriptions: well chilled, chilled, lightly chilled and room temperature. Learn the description for each style; the numbers are a guide.

    To keep wine cold at the table, use an insulated container or an ice bucket filled with ice and cold water; ice alone works less well. Never warm a red by a radiator, which damages its flavours: let it warm up naturally in the room where it will be served. These are recommendations only; if a guest prefers a different temperature, respect it.

    Glasses

    White & rosé

    Medium-sized glass, filled about halfway, to keep the wine cool and fresh.

    Red

    Larger bowl, filled up to the widest point, so aromas can gather.

    Sparkling

    Tall flute, about two-thirds full, to show off the bubbles and keep them longer.

    The best glasses have a bowl that narrows at the rim, which concentrates the aromas. Red wine glasses are usually slightly larger than white, and sparkling wine goes in a flute. Glasses must be completely clean and polished before use: dirt, finger marks or detergent residue can spoil a wine.

    Opening a bottle

    Still wine with a cork: remove the top of the capsule with a capsule remover or a waiter’s friend and clean the neck with a cloth. Centre the corkscrew in the middle of the cork, screw it in and draw the cork out gently, then wipe round the inside and outside of the neck. Sparkling wine is under high pressure, and even chilled, the cork can fly out and injure someone. From the moment you loosen the wire cage, keep the cork in your hand. Put these steps in the right order.

    Keeping an opened bottle

    Once opened, wine slowly goes stale as it reacts with air. Re-cork it and keep it in the fridge. For longer, keep the air away: a vacuum system uses a pump to remove air from the bottle, and a blanket system pumps in a gas that pushes the air out without harming the wine.

    Checking the wine before serving

    Before pouring, check the wine. Look: haziness could mean a fault, and there should be no bits floating in it. Smell: it should not smell stale, or of wet cardboard or vinegar.

    Cork taint

    Smells musty, like wet cardboard or a damp cellar, and the fruit is dull. Caused by a contaminated cork. It is harmless but unpleasant, and the bottle should be returned.

    Stale or vinegary

    From too much air, heat or light. The wine loses its fruity character and may smell stale or of vinegar.

    Module 5

    Food and wine

    Food changes how a wine tastes far more than wine changes food. The Level 1 course looks at seven things in food: sweetness, umami, acidity, salt, chilli heat, fat and flavour intensity.

    Choose a food component

    Green: the change usually helps the wine. Red: it usually makes the wine taste harsher or thinner.

    High risk and low risk

    High risk

    Sweetness and umami

    These make a wine taste harder: more drying, more bitter, more acidic and less fruity.

    Low risk

    Salt and acid

    These make a wine taste softer: smoother, fruitier and less acidic. They usually improve the match.

    Chilli heat, fat and flavour intensity are about balance. Chilli makes alcohol more noticeable (and alcohol makes the chilli hotter), fat makes a wine seem less acidic, and highly flavoured food can overwhelm a wine.

    Rules worth remembering

    If the food is…Choose a wine that is…
    Sweet (desserts)At least as sweet as the food, otherwise the wine tastes thin and sour.
    High in acid (lemon, tomato, vinaigrette)High in acidity, otherwise the wine tastes flat and flabby.
    Umami-rich (mushrooms, ripe cheese, cured meat)Fruity and not too tannic; adding salt or a squeeze of lemon to the dish helps.
    Spicy with chilliLow in alcohol, since chilli makes alcohol more noticeable. A medium-sweet German Riesling at 9% is a good choice.
    SaltyMost wines work; salt softens tannin and acidity. Try high-acid or sweet wines with salty food.
    Fatty or oily (cream sauces, fried food, rich meat)High in acidity to cut through the richness. Fat and protein also make tannic reds feel softer.
    Intensely flavouredEqually intense, so neither overpowers the other. Delicate food needs a delicate wine.

    Personal preference comes first. When recommending a wine, the most important thing is what that person likes. A textbook match is pointless if they don’t enjoy that style of wine. The rules simply show what most people would want to avoid.

      Module 6

      Tasting wine

      Tasting is a skill, and like any skill it gets better with a method. Look, smell, then taste, and describe each in the same words every time.

      Before you start

      Make sure there are no distracting smells, you have a white surface to judge the colour, a glass with a wide bowl that narrows towards the top to concentrate the aromas, and a clean palate with no lingering flavours such as toothpaste or coffee. Fill the glass about one-third full; water and plain crackers refresh the palate between wines.

      The Level 1 tasting approach: appearance (colour: white, rosé or red), nose (aromas of fruit, oak and other), and palate (sweetness, acidity, tannin, alcohol and body, plus flavours of fruit, oak and other).

      Look

      At Level 1 you only need to say whether a wine is white, rosé or red. For a head start on Level 2, tilt the glass against white and note the shade. Whites are lemon or gold; rosés are pink; reds are purple, ruby or garnet. Young reds tend to be purple; older reds move towards garnet. Whites get deeper in colour with age or oak.

      Smell

      Swirl the glass to release aromas, then take short sniffs. Group what you find: fruit first, then anything else such as flowers, herbs, spice or oak.

      Module 7

      Alcohol and health

      Enjoying wine responsibly means knowing how much alcohol you are drinking. The official workbook covers this briefly: in moderation alcohol is not regarded as harmful, but too much affects behaviour and both short- and long-term health, and it can be addictive. Low-risk guidelines vary from country to country. UK units are included here as useful background.

      One UK unit is 10 ml of pure alcohol. To work out the units in a drink, multiply the alcohol percentage (ABV) by the volume in millilitres, then divide by 1,000.

      Units = ABV % × volume (ml) ÷ 1,000
      Alcohol: 13%
      Serving

      Guidelines

      The UK Chief Medical Officers advise that, to keep health risks low, men and women should not regularly drink more than 14 units a week. If you drink that much, spread it evenly over three or more days and have several drink-free days. Guidelines differ between countries, so check the advice where you live.

      Driving

      Alcohol slows reactions and impairs judgement. The safest choice is not to drink at all if you are driving.

      Pregnancy

      The safest approach is not to drink alcohol at all.

      Pace

      Eat when you drink, alternate with water, and know that alcohol leaves the body slowly.

      Drinking too much over time raises the risk of serious harm, including liver disease, some cancers and heart problems, and it can affect relationships and work.
